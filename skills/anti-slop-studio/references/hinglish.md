# Hinglish and Hindi copy

Contents: register choice, native-writing rules, tells, Roman Hindi spelling, voiceover and TTS, review.

Basis: published practitioner guides agree that straight translation of English ad copy underperforms copy written natively, and that urban audiences respond to code-mixed Hinglish while formal Hindi can read as stiff. Specific CTR figures in vendor blogs are marketing claims; do not repeat them as fact. The rules below are craft heuristics. Have a native speaker of the target audience read anything important.

Terms: Roman Hindi describes the script (Hindi in Latin letters). Hinglish describes the language mix (Hindi and English together), which can be written in Roman, in Devanagari, or in both. There is no standard Roman spelling, so consistency inside one piece matters more than which spelling you pick.

## Choose the register first

| Register | Fits | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| Hinglish (Roman or Devanagari) | Urban and younger audiences, social reels, D2C | Over-English reads as alien outside metros |
| Pure Hindi (Devanagari) | Tier 2 and 3 cities, rural, older audiences | Sanskritised words sound like a government notice |
| Roman Hindi | Captions, chat-style UI, search behaviour | Spelling drifts unless one scheme is fixed |

Marketers also describe a third group: bilingual code-switchers who move between registers by context. Choose the script per surface (for example Devanagari for formal commerce and tier 2 and 3 audiences, Roman Hinglish for social and urban 18 to 35), and if it matters, test both on the same offer. Do not mix Devanagari and Roman Hindi inside one sentence. Write the choice and the reason in the direction brief. Regional languages (Marathi, Tamil, Bengali and others) are separate markets, not variants of Hindi.

## Write natively, never translate

1. Start from the viewer's situation in their words, not from an English line.
2. Code-switch at natural seams: nouns, tech and brand words stay English ("scroll", "reel", "order"); verbs, feelings and glue stay Hindi ("rok de", "bhaari pad gaya", "chal raha hai").
3. Keep sentences short. Spoken Hinglish runs in beats, not clauses.
4. Use one concrete local detail (a train platform, a power cut, a shaadi-season WhatsApp forward, cutting chai) only when it is true to the audience. A detail used as a costume is stereotype, not specificity.
5. Rhyme and parallel punchlines are native to Hindi copy and work well. Use them when the pair is genuinely surprising, not for every line.
6. Humour and idiom do not survive translation. If a line needs explaining, replace it.

## Tells in Hinglish copy

- **Translated CTAs:** "अभी खरीदें!", "आज ही संपर्क करें", "हमसे जुड़ें". Write what a person would say: "Chaliye, baat karte hain."
- **Translated English hooks:** "Kya aap jaante hain ki...", "क्या आपने कभी सोचा...", "Imagine a world...". Open with the situation or the tension.
- **Sanskritised register in casual copy:** उत्कृष्ट, सर्वोत्तम, अद्वितीय, अत्याधुनिक. Use the word people actually say.
- **Stock superlatives:** "sabse best", "number 1", "quality ka dusra naam", "ek naya andaz", "naye daur ki shuruaat". Replace with a proof point.
- **English slop inside Hinglish:** seamless, elevate, leverage, robust, next-level. The English rules in `writing.md` still apply to the Latin-script words.
- **Uniform tone:** every line the same excited pitch. Vary between a dry line, a warm line, and a sharp one.

## Roman Hindi spelling

Pick one scheme per piece and keep it. Common drift: nahi / nahin / nhi, bahut / bohot, kyun / kyu / kyon, accha / achha, mein / me. The scanner flags mixed variants. For on-screen text prefer the spelling the audience already types in chat.

## Voiceover and TTS

Practitioner guides for Hinglish voice and captions (mostly vendor blogs, so treat them as working conventions) agree on these:

- Keep one spelling per Hindi word throughout (not kya, kia and kyaa in one script).
- Keep narration sentences short, around 15 words or fewer, and use commas for breaths. Avoid long ellipsis chains.
- Write English brand names and product words in English spelling.
- Write amounts the way they should be spoken when it matters ("paanch sau rupaye", "do hazaar") and agree one house style for numbers in captions.
- Do not rely on automatic captions for names, slang, brand terms or numbers that switch between Hindi and English mid-phrase. Fix those by hand against the audio, and decide the script house style before checking.

- Write a voiceover script for the ear, not the eye. Spell difficult names phonetically for the engine, and test one line before generating the whole script.
- Keep a small pronunciation lexicon for brand and place names; synthetic voices commonly flatten aspirated consonants, misplace stress on English words inside Hindi sentences, and add awkward pauses at the switch point.
- Direct the delivery: where to breathe, which word carries the stress, where the beat of silence goes. Uniform emphasis is the voice equivalent of em-dash stacking.
- Prefer a short test render of the opening and the punchline, listened to without picture.

## Review checklist

- Would a person in this audience say this out loud?
- Is any line a word-for-word translation of an English idea?
- Is the spelling scheme consistent, and is each sentence in one script?
- Is each superlative backed by a proof point?
- Did a native speaker approve the one-liner and the CTA?
