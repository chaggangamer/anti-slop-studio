# Writing: structural tells and fixes

Contents: how to judge, the tells, rewrite method, buzzword note.

## How to judge

Slop is a density problem. One pattern is normal human writing. Several in one paragraph, repeated paragraph after paragraph, is what reads as machine. Writers trained in rhetoric, ESL writers and technical editors produce some of these patterns on purpose, so use the list as a mirror for the draft, never as proof of authorship.

## The tells

| ID | Tell | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| S01 | Contrast framing | "It's not a tool. It's a teammate." | It argues against a claim nobody made. State what the thing does. If both halves are real, join them: "faster and cheaper". |
| S02 | Throat-clearing opener | "In today's fast-paced world...", "Let's dive in" | Delete. Start at the first sentence that carries information. |
| S03 | Summary closer | "In conclusion...", a last line that restates the first with bigger words | Stop at the last real point. |
| S04 | Hedge stacking | "It's important to note that it may potentially help" | Commit, or say exactly what is uncertain and why. |
| S05 | Faux-insight setup | "Here's the thing", "That's the real unlock", "Let that sink in" | Say the insight itself. |
| S06 | Dramatic colon reveal | "The catch: nobody reads it." repeated | Colons are for lists, labels, quotes. Use a plain sentence. |
| S07 | Trailing -ing analysis | ", highlighting the importance of..." | Cut the clause or make it its own sentence with a fact. |
| S08 | Importance puffery | "a testament to", "plays a pivotal role", "game-changing" | Replace with the concrete effect, with a number if there is one. |
| S09 | Weasel attribution | "Experts say", "studies show" | Name the source or drop the claim. |
| S10 | Buzzword cluster | delve, tapestry, seamless, robust, leverage, elevate, navigate | Plain verb or noun. Judge clusters, not single words. |
| S11 | Em-dash habit | Several per paragraph doing different jobs | Commas, full stops, parentheses, or a new sentence. |
| S12 | Rule of three | Three adjectives, three benefits, three clauses by reflex | Use the real count. Three only when there are three. |
| S13 | Decoration | Bold on random words, emoji bullets, arrows | Let sentence structure carry emphasis. |
| S14 | Even rhythm | Every sentence the same length and shape | Vary length on purpose. One short sentence after a long one. |
| S15 | Colourful verb inflation | "drew 100 votes" instead of "got 100 votes" | Use the plain verb. |
| S16 | Placeholder or chatbot residue | "[Your Name]", "I hope this helps", "Great question" | Remove. These are the loudest tells of all. |
| S17 | Vague but confident | Specific-sounding claims with no number or source | Add the number, name, date or place, or cut. |
| S18 | False range | "from intimate gatherings to global movements" | Name the real items. A spectrum that is not a spectrum says nothing. |
| S19 | Hedging about source availability | "While details are limited...", "based on available information", "should be treated as X rather than Y" | Say what is known and cite it, or say plainly what is missing and stop. |
| S20 | Promotional or travel-guide language | "breathtaking", "rich cultural heritage", "must-visit", "enduring legacy" | Replace with a checkable detail: what it looks like, how many, since when. |
| S21 | Stacked transition words | moreover, furthermore, in addition, however, all in one paragraph | Cut most; let sentence order carry the logic. |
| S22 | Inline-header bullet lists | Four bullets that each begin with a bold label | Write prose, or use a table when it is real reference material. |
| S23 | Vague association | "widely associated with", "connected to" | State the actual relationship: who did what, when. |

## Rewrite method

1. **Delete first.** Openers, closers, hedges and announcements carry no information and cannot be "improved". Cut them and see what is left.
2. **State the second half.** For every contrast frame, drop the straw man and say the true half.
3. **Swap abstract for object.** "Streamlines workflows" becomes what actually happens: "sends the invoice when the job closes".
4. **Add one real detail** per paragraph: a name, number, place, constraint, or object the reader could check.
5. **Vary length.** Read it aloud. If it hums at one pitch, break the pattern.
6. **End on the last real point.** No aphorism, no tidy callback.

Example. Before: "It's not just a scheduler, it's a comprehensive content engine that seamlessly empowers teams to unlock their full potential." After: "It schedules posts and drafts replies for you to approve."

## When a tell is fine

These patterns were catalogued by editors trying to keep an encyclopedia neutral. Persuasive copy is allowed a point of view, so a real contrast ("we ship in two days, they ship in two weeks"), a confident claim you can back, or an aside that is really yours can be right. Keep a pattern when it is doing real work, and cut it when it is decoration.

Do not over-sanitise. Stripping every tell with a long list of prohibitions makes writing stiff and generic, which reads as more artificial, not less. Remove the few that matter in this piece, then add something only a person who did the work would know. Em-dash counts alone prove nothing: some careful human writers use them as often as models do, and some models use none.

## The check scripts cannot do

Structural signals tend to outlast word lists. After the scan, read the draft for what is missing:

- Claims with no named, dated source.
- Examples that are generic rather than specific (any company could say them).
- No firsthand detail: nothing only someone who did the work could write.
- A paragraph that describes the topic without adding anything to it.
- The point arriving late; the answer should be in the first lines.

## Plainer words (a starting map)

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| revolutionise | simplify, or say what changed |
| cutting-edge | practical, or name the feature |
| leverage | use |
| unlock value | help teams do X |
| world-class | proven, with the proof |

## Buzzword note

Word lists age fast because models are trained to avoid whatever becomes a meme. Treat vocabulary as a weak signal and structure as the strong one. When a banned word is replaced by its synonym and the sentence still says nothing, the problem was never the word.

## Short-form copy (captions, hooks, taglines)

- One idea per line. A hook that fits any product fits none.
- A tagline should be quotable aloud in one breath and impossible to paste onto a competitor.
- Rhyme, parallel structure and a surprising pairing are legitimate craft in a one-liner. Use them when they earn the line, not as a default shape.
- Read each line as the viewer's inner voice. If they would roll their eyes, rewrite.
