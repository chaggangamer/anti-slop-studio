# Concept and script

Contents: the brief, concept ladder, lenses, hooks, beat structure, specificity.

## The brief (decide before any generation)

- **Viewer:** exactly who, where they are, what they were doing a second ago.
- **Problem or question** that brought them here.
- **One idea** they should remember. If the sentence needs "and", it is two ideas; pick one.
- **Proof:** a real number, name, clip, screen or quote.
- **Action:** the single next step.

A script that could describe almost any company is the most common cause of generic video. Specificity at this stage cannot be added later by a better prompt.

## Concept ladder

Generate at least three concepts and place each on the ladder, then choose from the top rung that is true to the client.

1. **Literal:** shows the product or service doing its job. Safe, often forgettable.
2. **Category cliché:** the visual everyone in the category already uses (glowing globe, hands typing, handshake, "journey" montage, countdown intro, "Meet [Brand]"). Reject by default.
3. **Unexpected but true:** a surprising frame rooted in a real detail about the client, product or audience. This is the target.

## Lenses for finding rung three

- **Constraint:** what if the whole piece obeyed one strict rule (one colour, one word per beat, one continuous shot)?
- **Ritual inversion:** take a familiar ritual of the audience and bend one thing in it.
- **Scale shift:** show the idea at a wildly different size or time span.
- **Real artifact:** build the piece around a physical or digital object the client really has (a ledger, a clip, a form, a receipt).
- **Single object:** let one object carry the whole story.
- **Counter-position:** say what the category will not say about itself.

Test each concept with the swap test: replace the brand with a competitor. If it still works, go back to the brief and find what only this client can say.

## Hooks

- The first three seconds must give the viewer a reason: a question they care about, a tension, a visual they cannot place yet, or a fact that contradicts their assumption.
- Open on the middle of something, not on a logo, a greeting or a title card.
- The hook promises something the piece actually delivers. A bait hook trains viewers to leave.

## Beat structure for short pieces

| Length | Shape |
|---|---|
| 6 to 10 s | One image, one line, one turn. |
| 15 s | Hook (0 to 3), tension or proof (3 to 11), payoff plus line (11 to 15). |
| 30 s | Hook, setup, turn, proof, payoff, end card. |
| 60 s | Add a second beat of proof and a quiet moment before the turn. |

Ten to fifteen seconds has room for one idea. Three products, two locations and a call to action competing in one clip is the shape of slop.

## See and say

Do not let the voiceover say what the picture already shows ("You'll enjoy this pizza" over someone eating pizza). Let each channel carry something the other cannot: the picture shows, the line adds the meaning, the tension or the turn.

## End cards

End on what the viewer needs to act: name, one line, one contact path. A logo stuck on the end of an unrelated reel reads as an afterthought. Make the end card belong to the same visual system and keep it on screen long enough to read aloud once.

## Specificity ladder (for every line and every shot)

1. Generic claim ("fast, reliable service")
2. Quantified claim ("delivered in 48 hours")
3. Named instance ("the order that shipped before Friday")
4. A sensory image that proves it

Climb one rung higher than feels comfortable. If you have no real material for rungs three or four, ask for it, or say the claim is unproven rather than inventing business facts.

## Do not invent

Never fabricate testimonials, statistics, client names, prices, contact details or product behaviour. Use only information the user gave or that can be verified, and mark placeholders clearly so they cannot ship by accident.
